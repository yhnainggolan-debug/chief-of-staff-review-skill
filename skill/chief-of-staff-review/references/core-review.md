# Core review standard

## Start with the real job

Determine whether the artifact is meant to inform, align, persuade, explore, or secure approval. State the actual decision, commitment, or audience response sought. If no decision is requested, judge whether the artifact fulfills its narrower role rather than forcing an approval verdict.

## Build an evidence map

Classify material statements:

- **Fact:** supported by cited, traceable, or internally consistent evidence.
- **Assumption:** an input whose uncertainty matters.
- **Management claim:** asserted but not independently demonstrated.
- **Recommendation:** a proposed course of action.
- **Aspiration:** a desired future state.

Flag category errors, especially aspirations presented as achieved capabilities, targets presented as forecasts, and examples presented as pipeline.

## Two-axis readiness

### Artifact readiness

Judge whether the material performs its intended role:

- clear purpose and takeaway
- coherent structure and hierarchy
- appropriate density and level of detail
- credible wording and claim discipline
- effective delivery for its channel and audience

Use `ready`, `almost ready`, or `not ready`, with one-line reasoning.

### Decision sufficiency

Judge whether the underlying proposition supports action:

- decision and alternatives are explicit
- economics and value are supported
- assumptions and sensitivities are visible
- execution plan, owners, milestones, and dependencies exist
- material risks and mitigations are treated
- governance and requested authority are precise

Use `sufficient`, `partially sufficient`, or `insufficient`. Do not collapse the two axes into one verdict when they diverge.

## Materiality filter

Rank findings by consequence:

1. Could reverse or defer the decision.
2. Could materially change economics, risk, timing, or credibility.
3. Could impair execution or governance.
4. Could confuse the intended audience.
5. Pure polish—mention only if it materially affects comprehension or trust.

Prefer three decisive issues over fifteen observations.

## Decision architecture

Pressure-test:

- problem or opportunity and why now
- baseline and comparison case
- strategic fit and explicit trade-offs
- options considered and why the recommendation wins
- value creation logic and who captures it
- implementation path, owners, milestones, and dependencies
- downside, reversibility, and stop conditions
- exact decision, endorsement, or resource commitment requested

## Audience simulation

Adopt the audience's incentives and accountabilities, not just their title. A Chairman may test ambition, group logic, management credibility, and political consequences. An IC may test downside, assumptions, controls, and exit. A BU CEO may test autonomy, resource burden, and attribution.

Report:

- what lands quickly
- the first skeptical question
- the credibility gap
- the territorial or governance concern
- the minimum evidence or reframing needed to move forward

## Evidence anchors

Use the most precise anchor available:

- `(p. 12)` or `(slide 8)`
- `(beat 4)` or `(scene: partner gateways)`
- `(section: FY2026 bridge)`
- `(tab Demand, row 6)` or `(cell H14)`
- `(timestamp 03:20)`
- `("quoted phrase")`

If extraction loses pagination, say `(page unclear in extract)` once and use section or quote anchors thereafter. Never invent page numbers.

## Questions worth asking

Ask only questions whose answers could change the call. Favor questions about evidence, alternatives, downside, ownership, timing, governance, and decision scope. Avoid questions already answered or requests for detail with no decision relevance.

## Final call

End with one of:

- **Proceed:** evidence and execution are adequate.
- **Proceed with conditions:** name the conditions and owner.
- **Revise before decision:** specify the minimum revision.
- **Defer:** identify the missing evidence and next gate.
- **Reject:** explain the structural reason.
- **Continue development:** appropriate for early-stage concepts not yet seeking approval.

# Review Standards

Use these standards when reviewing decks, proposals, memos, board materials, business cases, and investment documents.

## Core Principle
The point is not to admire the deck. The point is to decide better.

Prioritize issues that would materially change:
- whether to proceed at all
- how much capital, time, or trust to commit
- how fast to move
- what risk needs mitigation first
- what management must prove before approval

Ignore cosmetic issues unless they hide a thinking problem.

## 1. Decision Clarity
First identify the actual decision being asked for.

Look for:
- the explicit ask
- required commitment: capital, people, management bandwidth, reputation, timing
- whether the ask is approval, budget, pilot, strategic direction, or simple feedback
- whether the document matches the decision stage

Red flags:
- many slides, no clear ask
- recommendation buried or implied
- analysis level mismatched to decision size
- deck asks for approval while still containing obvious unresolved basics

Questions to ask:
- What exact decision do you want, by when?
- What happens if we do nothing for 3 to 6 months?
- Which parts are already decided versus still open?

## 2. Strategy Quality
Check whether the recommendation has a coherent why-now and why-us logic.

Look for:
- strategic fit
- urgency with evidence, not theater
- competitive logic or positioning
- explanation of why this option is better than alternatives
- explicit tradeoffs

Red flags:
- trend-chasing
- generic phrases like "digital transformation", "synergy", "large market", "efficiency" without mechanics
- no alternatives considered
- no explanation of why now
- no capability advantage

Questions to ask:
- Why is this the right move now, not later?
- Why are we better positioned than others to win here?
- What alternatives were considered and why were they rejected?
- What must be true for this strategy to work?

## 3. Evidence Quality
Separate evidence from assertion.

Look for:
- source-backed facts
- triangulated claims
- real operating data, customer evidence, or market proof
- explicit assumptions with rationale

Red flags:
- conclusions that outrun evidence
- TAM slides pretending to be strategy
- benchmarks with no source or no comparability
- selective evidence showing upside only
- model precision far beyond the quality of inputs

Questions to ask:
- Which assumptions are facts, and which are management judgment?
- What evidence most strongly supports this recommendation?
- What evidence would make us change our mind?

## 4. Financial Review
Focus on whether the numbers are decision-useful, not merely present.

Look for:
- revenue bridge
- cost bridge
- margin logic
- cash impact
- capital requirements
- working-capital implications
- scenario analysis
- downside and break-even framing
- returns relative to risk and complexity

### Abnormal numbers tests
Flag as abnormal when numbers show patterns like these and the deck does not justify them clearly.

#### Growth abnormality
- revenue or volume growth that accelerates sharply without a matching channel, capacity, pricing, or sales-motion explanation
- steep growth immediately after launch without ramp assumptions
- market-share capture that seems too fast for the market structure
- international or adjacent expansion assumptions with no capability bridge

Questions:
- What operational engine creates this growth?
- What leading indicators would prove this ramp is real?
- What is the conservative case if adoption is half as fast?

#### Margin abnormality
- gross margin expansion that is too smooth or too fast
- EBITDA margin improvement without specific efficiency drivers
- margin improvement despite channel mix shift, discounting, or capex-heavy scaling
- top-line growth paired with impossible fixed-cost stability

Questions:
- Which levers drive margin expansion and when do they actually land?
- What costs are being assumed to stay flat, and why?
- What mix assumptions underpin the margin curve?

#### Profitability abnormality
- bottom line remains negative with no credible path to operating leverage
- bottom line turns positive abruptly with no bridge from gross profit to opex discipline
- profit growth far outpacing revenue growth without explicit operating leverage logic

Questions:
- What is the monthly or quarterly bridge to profitability?
- Is this an intentional investment phase or an economic problem?
- What has to improve first: conversion, pricing, utilization, or fixed-cost absorption?

#### Cost abnormality
- opex rises faster than top line with no capability build explanation
- headcount growth detached from revenue, output, or delivery plan
- marketing spend jumps without CAC / payback logic
- SG&A assumed flat in a scaling business
- maintenance, compliance, or support cost ignored in regulated or operationally heavy businesses

Questions:
- Which costs are step-function costs versus scalable costs?
- What cost line is most likely understated?
- How does headcount map to output, control, and service levels?

#### Cash / balance-sheet abnormality
- capex too low for the stated scale ambition
- working capital ignored in a growth plan
- debt service or covenant pressure not discussed
- cash burn acceptable only under heroic fundraising assumptions
- valuation implied by exit or returns math feels detached from fundamentals

Questions:
- What is the peak cash need and when does it occur?
- What balance-sheet constraints could kill this plan?
- Which assumption is most dangerous for liquidity?

#### Model-shape abnormality
- sudden inflection points with no operational milestone behind them
- perfectly smooth curves in messy businesses
- repeated round numbers where real modeling should be granular
- no seasonality where seasonality should exist
- no sensitivity despite obvious uncertainty

Questions:
- What specifically causes the inflection in this month or quarter?
- Why is volatility absent from this model?
- Show the sensitivity table for the 3 assumptions that matter most.

### Financial red flags
- upside case presented as base case
- no downside case
- EBITDA or net income cited without cash context
- returns discussed without time-to-value or risk weighting
- ratios shown without absolute numbers
- percentage growth emphasized over small base reality
- cumulative totals used to hide weak period performance

## 5. Execution Realism
A good idea with a fantasy execution plan is still a bad recommendation.

Look for:
- named owners
- milestones and sequence
- dependencies
- resourcing
- system/process implications
- operational bottlenecks
- implementation risk

Red flags:
- no owner
- unrealistic timeline
- assumes cross-functional alignment for free
- pilot success treated as scale readiness
- regulatory / technology / vendor dependencies hand-waved away

Questions to ask:
- Who owns each critical workstream?
- What are the gating dependencies?
- What breaks first if we scale faster than expected?
- What internal capability is missing today?

## 6. Risk and Downside
Serious materials explain what could go wrong.

Look for:
- downside case
- failure modes
- reversibility
- decision checkpoints
- mitigation plan
- triggers for stopping, slowing, or changing course

Red flags:
- risk slide with generic bullets only
- no scenario planning
- irreversible commitment hidden inside a “pilot”
- no kill criteria

Questions to ask:
- What is the most likely way this fails?
- What would we monitor monthly to catch that early?
- What part of the commitment is reversible, and what part is not?
- Under what conditions would we stop?

## 7. Governance and Attention
Surface what actually deserves executive attention.

Prioritize:
- assumptions that change the economics materially
- hidden dependencies on one person, one partner, one regulator, or one customer
- decisions that are hard to reverse
- reputational or compliance risk
- asks that quietly require much more management bandwidth than stated

Questions to ask:
- What issue here most deserves CEO or board attention?
- What issue should be delegated versus escalated?
- What has been made to look smaller than it really is?

## 8. Final Judgment Rules
Use these labels:

### Decision-ready
Use only when:
- the ask is clear
- numbers are coherent
- assumptions are visible
- downside is addressed
- owners and next steps are defined

### Almost ready
Use when:
- the direction is plausible
- only a few high-value gaps remain
- the missing pieces are answerable quickly

### Not ready
Use when:
- the ask is unclear
- evidence is weak
- financials are abnormal and unjustified
- execution depends on hand-waving
- downside is absent
- approving now would mainly reward confidence, not rigor

## 9. Sector Lenses
Apply these extra checks when the document matches the domain.

### Solar power and renewables
Look for:
- clear project economics, not just ESG language
- tariff, PPA, offtake, merchant-exposure, or subsidy assumptions stated plainly
- resource assumptions grounded in irradiation, wind profile, curtailment, degradation, and availability logic
- interconnection, permitting, land, EPC, OEM, and grid constraints surfaced early
- capex split, commissioning ramp, O&M, replacement cycles, and financing assumptions shown clearly
- downside cases for delay, curtailment, lower yield, weaker tariff, FX, and higher cost of capital

Red flags:
- IRR or payback claims with no tariff / yield / capex bridge
- heroic CUF, availability, or degradation assumptions
- smooth generation assumptions with no seasonality or curtailment risk
- financing terms that do all the work in the model
- “green demand” narrative substituting for bankable offtake
- no view on permitting, land, interconnection queue, or EPC delivery risk

Questions to ask:
- What 3 assumptions drive project IRR the most?
- What happens to DSCR / equity IRR under delay, lower yield, or higher capex?
- How bankable is the offtake and what are the termination / curtailment protections?
- What is the realistic COD risk and what dependencies sit outside our control?

### M&A
Look for:
- clear deal thesis and value-creation logic
- stand-alone quality versus synergy story kept separate
- purchase price logic tied to earnings quality, cash generation, and risk
- integration complexity acknowledged, not hidden
- downside case for under-delivery, customer loss, culture clash, key-person risk, and separation issues

Red flags:
- synergy-led thesis without strong stand-alone economics
- multiple expansion doing more work than operations
- aggressive revenue synergy with no commercial mechanism
- weak diligence on customer concentration, working capital, contingent liabilities, tax, compliance, or systems
- no integration owner or no Day 1 / 100-day plan

Questions to ask:
- If synergies fail, is this still a good deal?
- What are we truly buying: cash flow, capability, market access, or narrative?
- Which diligence finding could most change price or structure?
- What breaks first in integration?

### Mining
Look for:
- reserve, resource, grade, stripping, recovery, and throughput assumptions tied to operational reality
- commodity price assumptions separated clearly from operating performance assumptions
- mine plan, infrastructure, permitting, contractor, and logistics constraints made explicit
- capex, sustaining capex, haul distance, fuel, labor, maintenance, and royalty assumptions shown clearly
- downside cases for grade variability, weather, geotechnical issues, permitting, contractor performance, and commodity price weakness

Red flags:
- project economics that only work on optimistic commodity prices
- production ramps with no equipment, contractor, or infrastructure bridge
- all-in costs that look too clean or too low for the site reality
- reserve confidence and mine-life claims presented with more certainty than geology supports
- no discussion of permitting, community, ESG, or export/logistics bottlenecks

Questions to ask:
- How much of the value here is geology versus execution versus commodity price?
- What assumption is doing the most work in project NPV or IRR?
- What breaks first if prices soften or ramp-up slips?
- Which operational bottleneck is most likely in the first 12 months?

### Logistics
Look for:
- route density, utilization, fill rate, dwell time, turnaround time, and network design logic
- clear unit economics by shipment, trip, warehouse, lane, or customer segment
- service-level assumptions matched to labor, fleet, warehousing, and systems capacity
- fuel, maintenance, labor, claims, and working-capital effects represented honestly
- downside cases for volume volatility, customer concentration, disruption, and service failure

Red flags:
- revenue growth without utilization or route-density improvement logic
- margin targets that ignore fuel, labor, claims, empty miles, or warehouse inefficiency
- network expansion assumptions with no ramp, no local ops burden, and no service degradation risk
- customer concentration or key-lane dependency buried in the appendix
- software/platform narrative used to hide a hard operational business

Questions to ask:
- Which operational KPI actually drives margin here?
- What utilization level is assumed and how realistic is it?
- What happens if volume arrives but route density does not?
- Where do service failures or claims spike first in the plan?

### Financial services
Look for:
- clear economics by product, cohort, customer segment, or distribution channel
- explicit regulatory, compliance, underwriting, fraud, capital, or liquidity assumptions
- revenue growth tied to acquisition, conversion, retention, pricing, balance sheet, or cross-sell mechanics
- credit, claims, cost of funds, reserve, or capital consumption reflected honestly where relevant
- downside cases for regulation, defaults, fraud, market shocks, liquidity, and reputational risk

Red flags:
- growth assumptions detached from risk, compliance, funding, or capital constraints
- margin claims that ignore loss rates, reserves, cost of funds, or servicing cost
- customer growth that outruns underwriting, operations, or compliance capability
- “platform” or “ecosystem” claims that obscure weak unit economics
- no treatment of concentration, conduct risk, or regulatory dependency

Questions to ask:
- What regulatory or capital constraint matters most here?
- What happens if defaults, fraud, or claims normalize above plan?
- Which economics are real before marketing support and accounting presentation?
- What part of the model is most sensitive to trust, funding, or compliance failure?

### B2B enterprise software
Look for:
- clear ICP, buying motion, implementation burden, and expansion path
- revenue quality: ARR, gross retention, net retention, churn, logo concentration, ACV mix
- sales efficiency: CAC payback, pipeline quality, win rates, ramp time, sales capacity logic
- product reality: deployment time, integration complexity, services dependence, roadmap credibility
- margin logic that reflects hosting, support, implementation, partner economics, and enterprise success cost

Red flags:
- ARR growth with weak retention or poor gross margin
- enterprise claims without proof of repeatable sales motion
- huge pipeline story without conversion evidence
- AI / platform / ecosystem language with no wedge, no ROI proof, or no adoption proof
- low churn claimed on too-small or too-young a customer base
- services-heavy implementation hidden under software narrative

Questions to ask:
- What is the real repeatable motion here, not the founder-hustle version?
- How much revenue quality comes from expansion versus replacement selling?
- What implementation or integration friction slows scale?
- If hiring slows, what happens to growth next year?

## 10. Recent Headlines Check
When the review topic is tied to a sector, geography, company, commodity, regulation, customer type, or strategic theme that could be affected by current events, search the web for important headlines from the last 7 days.

Use this check to pressure-test whether the document is already stale or missing a live risk/opportunity.

Look for headlines about:
- commodity price moves
- regulation or policy changes
- M&A activity or competitor moves
- financing conditions, rates, or liquidity shifts
- major accidents, outages, sanctions, or legal issues
- customer demand shifts or procurement changes
- technology/platform changes that alter assumptions

Rules:
- include only items that materially affect the review
- prefer a few relevant headlines over a noisy list
- connect each headline back to the deck's assumptions or timing
- if nothing important is found, say that clearly instead of forcing it

## 11. Scoring Rubric
Use these scores when the skill output includes scoring.

### 9 to 10
Decision-grade. Clear ask, strong evidence, coherent economics, visible assumptions, real downside framing, and credible execution ownership.

### 7 to 8
Solid but not clean. Core direction may be right, but there are still gaps worth pressing on before approval.

### 5 to 6
Mixed quality. Material contains useful substance but too much is still unsupported, incomplete, or weakly framed.

### 3 to 4
Weak. Significant gaps in logic, evidence, economics, or execution realism. Not approval-ready.

### 1 to 2
Dangerous. The material is mostly confidence theater, narrative inflation, or broken logic.

When scoring:
- do not average mechanically if one category is a real deal-breaker
- a polished deck with weak economics should still score badly
- if evidence quality is poor, confidence level should usually be low

## 12. In-the-Room Question Style
Questions asked live should:
- be short enough to deliver in one breath
- force specificity
- expose unsupported assumptions quickly
- test ownership, downside, timing, and economic logic
- avoid sounding like consultant homework

Good examples:
- What has to be true for this to work?
- Which assumption here is doing the most work?
- If we are wrong, where do we see it first?
- Why is this base case not actually an upside case?
- If synergies fail, is this still worth doing?
- What breaks first if rollout is slower or more expensive?
- Who owns this on day one?
- What would make us regret approving this today?

## 13. Decision Memo Rewrite Rule
If the material is directionally sensible but poorly argued, rewrite the decision spine rather than merely criticizing formatting.

The rewrite should clarify:
- the actual decision requested
- the 2 to 4 arguments that justify it
- the economics that matter most
- the main risks and what would mitigate them
- what evidence is still missing before approval

## 14. Review Tone
- Be direct.
- Be skeptical without becoming theatrical.
- Do not praise polish over substance.
- Call bullshit when numbers or logic are clearly weak, but explain why.
- Separate critical concerns from nice-to-have improvements.
- Prefer crisp bullets and sharp questions over long narrative.
